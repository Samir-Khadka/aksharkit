# Sources and decisions

## 1. Number words (0–100)

The table in `src/aksharkit/words.py` was typed by hand and compared, entry by
entry, with two full published lists read on 23 November 2026:

1. Omniglot, "Numbers in Nepali" — <https://www.omniglot.com/language/numbers/nepali.htm>
2. Lingocard, "Nepali numbers" — <https://lingocard.com/nepali-numbers> (also lists "also written" variants)

Partial corroboration (individual words seen, not full lists):

- Wikipedia, "Numbers in Nepali language" — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numbers_in_Nepali_language>
- <https://www.imnepal.com/nepali-numbers/>
- <https://ling-app.com/blog/nepali-numbers-and-counting/>
- <https://barala.com.np/calculators/nepali-numbers-and-words/>

These are web references, **not** an official dictionary or the Nepal
Academy's spelling rules. No such authority was consulted. A native speaker
should review the table.

`tests/golden/number_words_0_100.tsv` was generated from the reviewed table.
It guards against accidental changes; it is not independent proof.

## 2. Stopwords

`src/aksharkit/stopwords.py` is a short list of closed-class words
(postpositions, pronouns, conjunctions, common auxiliary forms) written by
hand for this project. It was **not** copied from another package or corpus
and has not been reviewed by a native speaker or measured against a corpus.

## 3. Normaliser

Behaviour follows the Unicode Standard: NFC as defined in UAX #15, and the
Devanagari block's guidance that ZWJ/ZWNJ affect conjunct shaping after a
virama. The list of split-vowel repairs was written from the Devanagari code
chart's "do not use" sequences as recalled; it was not re-checked against the
chart text during this build.

## 4. Preeti key table — experimental, compared but not checked against documents

From 0.6 `src/aksharkit/data/font_preeti.json` holds a table, labelled
**experimental — mapping agreed by at least 2 of 3 permissively-licensed
table families (4 projects), not yet checked against real Preeti documents
or by a native reader**. The full account, with the agreement counts, the
keys where tables differ and the reordering rules, is in
[preeti.md](preeti.md). Version 0.7 changed no entry of the table and
consulted no new source: its Word-file handling, protection rules and
Unicode → Preeti direction are aksharkit's own code on top of the same table,
and the reverse direction is derived from the table, not from another
converter. In short:

- On 24 November 2026 the Preeti tables of four MIT-licensed projects were
  read as text at pinned commits: nepali-unicoder, nepalikit,
  preeti-unicode-converter and nepali-keyboard. nepali-unicoder and nepalikit
  share one rule list and count as one family, so there are three families.
- 136 single keys were compared. 122 are agreed by two or more families with
  no table differing; 4 are agreed by two or more families with one table
  differing (used by default, off with `strict`); 5 appear in one family only
  (off by default); 5 have no agreement and are never used.
- The table was written from that comparison. No project's file, code or
  rule list was copied; the reordering code is aksharkit's own. The values
  each project states are kept, with attribution, in
  `scripts/preeti_sources.json`, and `scripts/compare_preeti_sources.py`
  repeats the comparison. The four copyright notices are in `NOTICE`.
- nepali-converter (nepalibhasha/nepali-fonts) was **excluded**: the header
  of its Preeti map names npttf2utf (GPL-3.0) as a reference.
- npttf2utf, foss-np/2utf8, npm `preeti` and sapradhan/nep-ttf2utf were not
  opened. The three small npm packages were not read beyond their registry
  metadata.

**Still open:** none of the four projects says where its table came from, and
their authors have not been asked. Whether any of them descends from the GPL
or non-commercial tables is therefore unknown. No real Preeti text has been
converted and no native reader has reviewed the table.

Other findings:

- **The Preeti font itself.** Web pages commonly attribute it to Ajay Mishra,
  1993, and call it freeware
  ([nepaltype.com](https://www.nepaltype.com/blogs/what-is-preeti-font),
  [topnepali.com](https://topnepali.com/top-nepali-fonts)); one of the same
  sites names a different designer on another page. No licence text for the
  font was found. aksharkit does not need to ship the font, only a table of
  which key shows which letter.
- **No standards document** for the Preeti layout was found. Pages describe it
  as following the Remington typewriter layout and as the model for the Nepali
  Unicode "Traditional" keyboard layout; neither claim was checked against a
  primary source.
- **The licence label is not the whole story.** The oldest public rule sets
  (2utf8, npttf2utf, npm `preeti`) are GPL or non-commercial. The newer
  MIT-labelled packages do not say whether their tables were typed up
  independently or carried over from those. That question was not answered,
  so none of them counts as "clearly MIT-compatible" yet.

### What was recommended, and where it stands

1. Real Preeti text with the correct Unicode beside it, as the golden test
   set. **Not done** — needs Samir or another Preeti typist. The empty file
   `tests/golden/preeti_samples.tsv` is waiting for it.
2. Compare at least two MIT-licensed tables and keep what agrees. **Done in
   0.6** with three families. Asking the authors where their tables came
   from (a GitHub issue each) is **not done**; it needs Samir's agreement.
3. Reordering rules in aksharkit's own code. **Done in 0.6**, but driven by
   self-made samples, not by golden tests, because step 1 is still open.
4. Mark the table unreviewed until a native reader has read it. **Done**: it
   is labelled experimental everywhere it is shown.

## 5. Bikram Sambat calendar table — verified for BS 2000–2083

BS month lengths are published year by year and cannot be computed.
`src/aksharkit/data/bs_calendar.json` holds the lengths for **BS 2000 to 2083
(14 April 1943 to 13 April 2027)**. It was built on 23 November 2026 by
comparing five published tables and keeping the years on which they agree.

### What the comparison found

| Years (BS) | Result | In the table? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1970–1999 | Two sources only (nepali-datetime from 1975, medic); they disagree on 1975, 1989, 1991 and 1993 | No |
| 2000–2061 | All five agree exactly | Yes |
| **2062** | nepali-datetime has Baishakh 31, Jestha 31; the other four have Baishakh 30, Jestha 32. The other ten months and the 365-day total agree | **Yes, with a guard — see below** |
| 2063–2083 | All five agree exactly | Yes |
| 2084–2090 | py-nepali differs from the other four in every year | No |
| 2091–2099 | Only nepali-datetime and py-nepali reach this far and they disagree; nepali-datetime's 2096 adds up to 364 days | No |

Other checks that passed: every included year has 12 months of 29 to 32 days
and totals 365 or 366; walking medic's own starting point (BS 1970-01-01 =
13 April 1913) forward lands on BS 2000-01-01 = 14 April 1943, the same day
py-nepali states; the 84 years add up to exactly the 30,681 days between
14 April 1943 and 13 April 2027.

### The disputed month in 2062

The task for this build was "include only years where the sources agree
exactly". 2062 does not meet that. Leaving it out would have cut the table in
two, so it is included with the majority values and a guard: any date from
**31 Baishakh to 32 Jestha 2062 (14 May to 14 June 2005)** raises
`DisputedDateError` unless the caller passes `allow_disputed=True`. Dates
outside those 32 days are the same under both readings.

Outside evidence is split as well, so the question is open:

- Wikipedia's "List of massacres in Nepal" and "Maoist insurgency in Nepal"
  give 6 June 2005 as 24 Jestha 2062 — this fits the majority reading.
- Advocacy Forum press statements give the same day as 23 Jestha 2062 — this
  fits nepali-datetime's reading.

A printed 2062 calendar would settle it.

## Romanised Nepali word list (`aksharkit.detect`)

`ROMANISED_NEPALI_WORDS` (71 words) was written by hand for this project
from general knowledge of how Nepali is typed in Latin letters: common verb
forms with their `ch`/`chh`/`x` spellings (cha, chha, xa, chaina, xaina,
garchu, garxu, huncha, hunxa, bhayo, vayo, thiyo), pronouns (timi, tapai,
hami, mero, hamro, malai, timilai), question words (kasto, kati, kina,
kahile, kasari) and a few others (ramro, dherai, pani, sanga, dekhi, bata,
aja, hijo, bholi). Words that are also common English words, or that
romanised Hindi clearly shares, were left out on purpose. It was not taken
from any corpus or other project, it has not been reviewed by a native
speaker, and the heuristic built on it has not been measured.

## Ordinals 1–100 (`ordinal_to_words`, added in 0.5)

**No source was consulted.** The forms below were written from general
knowledge and by one rule, and every one of them needs a native speaker's
eye before it is relied on.

- Six forms are listed by hand: पहिलो (1), दोस्रो (2), तेस्रो (3), चौथो (4),
  छैटौँ (6), नवौँ (9).
- Every other form is the package's cardinal plus the ending: the sign `ौँ`
  when the cardinal ends in a consonant (पाँच → पाँचौँ, दस → दसौँ, सय → सयौँ),
  and the letter `औँ` when it ends in a vowel sign (साठी → साठीऔँ, असी →
  असीऔँ, नब्बे → नब्बेऔँ). That second case covers 32 numbers, 59–70 and
  79–98, and is the part least likely to be right: forms such as साठीऔँ,
  सत्तरीऔँ, असीऔँ and नब्बेऔँ may well be written differently in practice.
- The ending is spelt with candrabindu (`-औँ`), not anusvara (`-औं`).
- Each form inherits the cardinal's spelling, so the open choices in
  section 1 carry over (दसौँ rather than दशौँ, बिसौँ rather than बीसौँ,
  उनान्सयौँ for 99).
- Only this one base form is produced. Nothing is claimed about feminine,
  plural or oblique forms (पहिली, पहिला and so on), and ordinals are not read
  back by `parse_words`.

## Alphabetical order (`sort_key`, added in 0.5)

No dictionary or official rule was consulted. `docs/sorting.md` lists every
choice. The ones most worth a native speaker's check:

1. `क्ष`, `त्र`, `ज्ञ` as letters after `ह`, including in the middle of a word
   (`कक्षा` after `कह…`), with `conjunct_letters=False` to file them under
   `क`, `त`, `ज` instead.
2. candrabindu, then anusvara, then visarga, each directly after the bare
   syllable and before any following letter (`कँ < कं < कः < कक < का`).
3. Anusvara is not treated as a nasal consonant (`अंक` and `अङ्क` sort apart).
4. Conjuncts after every vowel sign of the same consonant (`कौ < क्या`,
   `करा < कर्म`).
5. Nukta letters with the plain letter, plain first.
6. Digits, punctuation and Latin before Devanagari; digit strings compared
   digit by digit.

## Aksharas (`aksharas`, added in 0.5)

The rules in `docs/aksharas.md` were written from the Unicode Devanagari
block chart's structure, not from another segmenter. Choices to check: a
final halant consonant is its own unit (श्री, मा, न्); spaces, digits and
punctuation each count as one.

## Date patterns and month-name parsing (added in 0.5)

The tokens of `format_bs` patterns are this project's own small subset in
the style of `strftime`. `%a` uses the short weekday names in the section
above. `parse_bs` accepts only the month spellings the package writes, plus
the particles साल after the year and गते after the day; the other spellings
listed under "Month and weekday names" are refused, not guessed.

## Normaliser: `र्इ` → `ई` (added in 0.5)

The repair rests on this project's own reasoning that र + virama directly
followed by the independent vowel इ has no other reading in Nepali text and
is a common way of mistyping ई. It was not checked against a corpus.
